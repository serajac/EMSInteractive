Section 1 · Introduction & Background

The AMLS Assessment Pathway

Trauma has algorithms. Cardiac arrest has algorithms. Medical patients—the majority of EMS responses—deserve the same disciplined approach. AMLS gives you that pathway.

Why AMLS Exists

Most EMS clinicians are highly proficient with trauma and cardiac arrest because both have well-rehearsed protocols. Medical patients are a different challenge: the chief complaint is often vague ("weakness," "not feeling right," "abdominal pain"), the differential is wide, and time-critical conditions hide inside non-specific presentations. AMLS, developed by NAEMT, gives prehospital providers a structured, hypothesis-driven framework so the working diagnosis drives the treatment plan—not the other way around.

The course originated from the recognition that field impressions matter: research demonstrates that EMS-generated working diagnoses are increasingly used by ED triage, pre-arrival labs, cardiac cath team activation, and stroke center bypass decisions. Sloppy field assessment costs minutes, and minutes cost outcomes.

Why This Matters on Shift

Within 90 seconds of patient contact, you should be answering three questions:

  1. Sick or not sick?
  2. What's the leading hypothesis?
  3. What's the time-critical thing I cannot miss?

The AMLS pathway is the discipline that gets you there.

The Seven-Step Pathway

  1. Scene size-up
  2. Initial / primary impression
  3. Detailed assessment (history + focused exam)
  4. Working diagnosis
  5. Treatment / intervention
  6. Re-evaluation / ongoing assessment
  7. Transport & handoff

Note: each step includes a differential refinement. The diagnosis sharpens with every data point.

Section 2 · Core Concepts

Building the Working Diagnosis

The AMLS pathway treats clinical reasoning as a cycle of refinement. Each finding either strengthens or weakens a hypothesis. The clinician's job is to know which findings shift probability.

Step 1 — Scene Size-Up

Before you touch the patient, the scene tells a story. Pill bottles, environment, family interactions, the smell of the room—all are diagnostic data. Document anything that influences differential: empty insulin pens (DKA/hypoglycemia), prescription opioids (overdose), incontinence (seizure), vomit pattern (coffee-grounds = upper GI bleed).

Step 2 — Primary Impression (Sick / Not Sick)

The primary impression is a gestalt—a clinical first read driven by the AVPU level, work of breathing, skin signs, and the patient's general appearance. Trust it. If your first impression is "this person is dying," that bias should drive aggressive early action while you confirm.

Use the ABCDE approach in this order:

  • A – Airway patency & protection
  • B – Breathing (rate, work, SpO₂, lung sounds, EtCO₂)
  • C – Circulation (pulse, BP, perfusion, bleeding)
  • D – Disability (GCS, BGL, pupils, gross motor)
  • E – Exposure / environment

Step 5 — Treatment Pearls by Cardinal Complaint

Dyspnea

  • Pulmonary edema (cardiogenic): CPAP early, NTG SL/IV/drip if SBP >110, treat underlying ischemia. Avoid loop diuretics if BP unclear—many of these patients are euvolemic with redistribution.
  • Severe asthma/COPD: Albuterol/ipratropium nebulized, IV/IM steroid (methylprednisolone 125 mg or dexamethasone 10 mg), IM epinephrine if peri-arrest, magnesium sulfate 2 g IV in severe.
  • PE: No prehospital fix beyond support — high-flow O₂, gentle fluids if hypotensive (avoid overload), early notification.

Anaphylaxis

  • Epinephrine 0.3–0.5 mg IM (1 mg/mL) anterolateral thigh — first drug, every encounter. Repeat q5–15 min if needed.
  • IV fluids 1–2 L NS for hypotension; H₁ (diphenhydramine 25–50 mg), H₂ (famotidine), steroid for biphasic prevention.
  • If refractory: epinephrine infusion 0.1 mcg/kg/min titrated.

Sepsis

  • Recognize: SIRS criteria + suspected source, OR qSOFA ≥2 (RR ≥22, AMS, SBP ≤100).
  • Crystalloid 30 mL/kg titrated to perfusion, early notification for sepsis-alert receiving facility, antibiotics if local protocol, vasopressors (norepinephrine) once volume is in.

Hypoglycemia

  • D10W 100–250 mL IV (preferred over D50 in many systems — gentler on veins, less rebound).
  • Glucagon 1 mg IM if no IV access.
  • Recheck BGL in 10 min; consider thiamine 100 mg IV in malnourished patients before glucose if Wernicke's risk.

Stroke

  • BE-FAST (Balance, Eyes, Face, Arm, Speech, Time of last known well).
  • Bypass to CSC for LVO if <24 hr from LKW; PSC for non-LVO <4.5 hr.
  • Permissive HTN (don't treat unless >220/120 or symptomatic).

Toxicology

  • Opioid: Naloxone 0.4–2 mg IV/IM/IN titrated to respirations (not LOC).
  • TCA: Sodium bicarbonate 1–2 mEq/kg IV bolus for QRS >100 ms or hypotension.
  • Beta-blocker / CCB: Calcium, glucagon (high-dose), high-dose insulin/dextrose ("HIET") in protocol.
  • Organophosphate: Atropine — large repeat doses to dry secretions; pralidoxime per protocol.
Self-test. Can you state, from memory, three differentials for new-onset dyspnea in a 65-year-old with COPD? If you stopped at "exacerbation," you've missed PE, pulmonary edema, pneumonia, ACS, and pneumothorax. Stay broad until your data narrows you.

Section 4 · Practical Application

Case Study — "Just Not Feeling Right"

Dispatch

1737 hrs — 68-year-old female, "general weakness," lives alone in a single-family home. No prior calls today.

Scene

Patient seated upright at the kitchen table. Pill organizer on counter showing today's morning dose untaken. Empty glass and a half-full cup of water. Apartment is warm, no smoke or odor. Daughter on speaker phone — concerned because mom "sounds confused."

Patient verbatim: "I just don't feel right. I felt funny since lunchtime. My arm is heavy."

Initial Impression
AVPUA×3 (slow)
SkinCool, diaphoretic
Resp22, mild WOB
HR96 irreg
BP108/64
SpO₂94% RA
EtCO₂32 mmHg
BGL104 mg/dL
Temp36.8 °C

Hx: HTN, DM2, paroxysmal AFib (on apixaban). Meds also include metoprolol, lisinopril, metformin. Last meal: light lunch ~1230. Allergies: NKDA. Smoker, 1 ppd × 40 yr. Family hx: father MI at 62.

Pause & Differentiate

Before reading on, write down your top 3 differentials. Notice what data points pulled you toward each.

Reveal differential refinement

Top differentials:

  1. Acute coronary syndrome — atypical female presentation. "Heavy arm," diaphoresis, irregular HR, age, DM, smoker, family hx. Women, diabetics, and elderly often lack classic crushing substernal chest pain.
  2. Stroke — heaviness on one side could be hemiparesis. Confusion + cool/diaphoretic skin can be cerebral. Need BE-FAST.
  3. Sepsis — atypical AMS with subtle vital changes. AFib could be a sepsis-driven dysrhythmia.
  4. Other: hypoglycemia (ruled out by BGL 104), DKA (BGL too low), PE (would have lower SpO₂ usually), aortic dissection (no chest/back pain yet).
Detailed Exam
  • Cranial nerves: subtle left facial droop noted on smile. Speech mildly slurred (daughter confirms this is new).
  • Arms: pronator drift on left.
  • Lung sounds: clear bilaterally.
  • Abdomen: soft, non-tender.
  • 12-lead: Atrial fibrillation, rate 92, <1 mm ST depression V4–V6, no STEMI criteria. No new bundle branch block.
  • BE-FAST: positive for face/arm/speech. Last known well = ~1230 (~5 hr ago).
Reasoning & Action

The pattern—new focal neurologic deficit + AFib + anticoagulant on board + AMS within stroke window for some interventions but outside tPA window—now points to acute ischemic stroke, possibly LVO. The "heavy arm" was hemiparesis, not ACS. The mild ST changes are demand or rate-related, not the primary problem.

Plan:

  • Permissive HTN; do not lower BP.
  • Maintain SpO₂ >94%, head of bed neutral or ~30°.
  • Glucose verified normal—no D₅₀.
  • NPO. IV access × 2.
  • Bypass to Comprehensive Stroke Center (LVO suspicion + LKW within thrombectomy window of 24 hr per current guidelines).
  • Stroke alert called en route; LKW time, anticoagulant use, and BE-FAST score communicated.

Debrief Questions

  1. What initial finding most strongly should have pulled you toward neurologic before cardiac? (The "heavy arm" + slow speech in an AFib patient on anticoagulation.)
  2. Why does the apixaban matter? (Anticoagulation expands the differential to include intracerebral hemorrhage; CT before any intervention.)
  3. Where would you have erred toward over-treatment if you'd anchored on ACS? (Aspirin loading—relatively contraindicated until ICH excluded; nitroglycerin—could worsen cerebral perfusion.)
Section 5 · Summary & Key Takeaways

What to Carry Into Your Next Shift

Five Anchors

  1. Pathway over protocol. The AMLS pathway is a way of thinking. Protocols are tools you reach for once the pathway has narrowed your differential.
  2. Sick or not sick is the first decision. Make it inside 90 seconds. Be willing to revise.
  3. Atypical is typical. Women, diabetics, and the elderly rarely read the textbook. Lean into the broad differential when the chief complaint is vague.
  4. Treat what kills first. Hypoxia, hypotension, hypoglycemia, hyper/hypothermia, and acidosis—correct these before chasing definitive diagnosis.
  5. Handoff drives outcomes. A clean MIST report can save 20 minutes of door-to-balloon, door-to-needle, or door-to-antibiotic.

Module Outcomes — ACLS

Advanced Cardiac Life Support

The American Heart Association's ACLS course translates the latest resuscitation science into a small set of memorized algorithms, role-defined teamwork, and high-quality CPR. This review aligns with the 2020 AHA Guidelines and 2023 Focused Update.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this ACLS review, the learner will be able to:

1
Identifyshockable (VF/pVT) and non-shockable (asystole, PEA) cardiac arrest rhythms and recall the corresponding algorithm steps, drug doses, and timing intervals.
2
Explainthe components of high-quality CPR—rate, depth, recoil, minimal interruption, and ventilation rate—and the physiologic rationale for each.
3

Bloom alignment. ACLS is rule-bound by design—algorithms enforce consistency under stress. The challenge is moving past recall to the analyze and create tier where leadership and reversible-cause hunting live.

Section 1 · Introduction & Background

The ACLS Framework

ACLS is the structured continuation of high-quality BLS, layering airway, IV/IO access, rhythm-specific drugs, and team leadership onto the foundation of fast, deep, full-recoil compressions with minimal interruption.

The Two Surveys

Every ACLS encounter starts with the BLS survey, then transitions to the ACLS survey:

BLS SurveyACLS Survey
C – Check responsiveness, pulse, breathingA – Airway (advanced if needed)
A – Activate emergency responseB – Breathing (waveform capnography, ventilation rate)
B – Begin CPR (30:2 until advanced airway)C – Circulation (rhythm, IV/IO, drugs)
D – Defibrillate as soon as availableD – Differential diagnosis (H's & T's)

High-Quality CPR Targets

  • Rate: 100–120 / min
  • Depth: ≥ 2 inches (5 cm), not > 2.4 inches (6 cm)
  • Recoil: Full chest recoil between compressions
  • Interruptions: < 10 seconds, ideally < 5
  • Compression fraction: > 60%, target ≥ 80%
  • Ventilation: 1 breath every 6 sec (10/min) once advanced airway placed; 30:2 before
  • EtCO₂: > 10 mmHg sustained suggests adequate compressions; rise to > 35 mmHg often heralds ROSC

The Five Algorithms

  1. Adult Cardiac Arrest (VF/pVT & Asystole/PEA)
  2. Adult Bradycardia (HR < 50 with symptoms)
  3. Adult Tachycardia (HR > 150 with symptoms)
  4. Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)
  5. Suspected Stroke

Plus: Post–Cardiac-Arrest Care, Opioid-Associated Emergency, Adult Immediate Post-Arrest pathway.

Section 2 · Core Concepts

Algorithms, Drugs & Decisions

Memorizing the boxes is not enough; you must memorize the order, timing, and rationale. The pace of your patient's recovery is set by how fluidly the team executes these steps.

Adult Cardiac Arrest — Shockable (VF / pulseless VT)

  1. Start CPR (rate 100–120, depth ≥ 2 in, full recoil), apply pads/monitor, attach EtCO₂, get IV/IO access.
  2. Rhythm check: VF / pVT → shock immediately. Biphasic 120–200 J (manufacturer-specific) or 360 J monophasic.
  3. Resume CPR for 2 minutes, prepare epinephrine.
  4. Rhythm check: still VF/pVT → shock at maximal energy. Resume CPR.
  5. Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 min; consider advanced airway with continuous waveform capnography.
  6. Rhythm check: still VF/pVT → shock; resume CPR; consider amiodarone 300 mg IV/IO bolus (second dose 150 mg) or lidocaine 1–1.5 mg/kg IV/IO (then 0.5–0.75 mg/kg).
  7. Continue cycles. Treat reversible causes (H's & T's). Use EtCO₂, arterial line if available, pulse checks at 2-min rhythm checks.
  8. If ROSC: post-cardiac-arrest care.

Adult Cardiac Arrest — Non-Shockable (Asystole / PEA)

  1. Start CPR, apply pads, EtCO₂, IV/IO.
  2. Confirm rhythm in 2 leads (asystole "flatline" should be verified).
  3. Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO ASAP, then every 3–5 min. Earlier epinephrine in non-shockable rhythms is associated with improved outcomes.
  4. Continue 2-min CPR cycles with rhythm checks.
  5. Aggressively pursue reversible causes—PEA almost always has a treatable cause (hypovolemia, hypoxia, tamponade, tension PTX, PE, MI, hyper-/hypokalemia, toxins).
  6. Consider POCUS during pulse check to assess wall motion and rule out pericardial effusion / tamponade.
  7. If ROSC: post-cardiac-arrest care.

Adult Bradycardia (HR < 50 with symptoms)

  1. Identify: HR < 50 plus signs of poor perfusion (hypotension, AMS, ischemic chest discomfort, signs of shock).
  2. Maintain airway, oxygen if hypoxic, IV/IO, monitor, 12-lead.
  3. Atropine 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 min, max 3 mg. (Note: 2020 update increased dose from 0.5 mg.)
  4. If atropine ineffective: transcutaneous pacing and/or dopamine 5–20 mcg/kg/min or epinephrine infusion 2–10 mcg/min.
  5. Treat underlying cause; consider expert consult / transvenous pacing.

Caution: atropine is unlikely to work in 2nd-degree Mobitz II or 3rd-degree AV block with wide-complex escape — go directly to pacing or chronotropic infusion.

Adult Tachycardia (HR > 150 with symptoms)

  1. Identify and stabilize ABCs, oxygen, IV/IO, monitor, 12-lead.
  2. Decide: STABLE or UNSTABLE? Unstable = hypotension, AMS, ischemia, signs of shock, acute heart failure.
  3. Unstable → synchronized cardioversion. Start at 100 J for narrow regular, 200 J for AFib, 200 J for monomorphic VT, defib doses for polymorphic VT.
  4. Stable narrow regular (SVT) → vagal maneuvers → adenosine 6 mg rapid IV push, then 12 mg, then 12 mg.
  5. Stable narrow irregular (AFib/AFlutter) → diltiazem 0.25 mg/kg IV (then 0.35 mg/kg), or beta-blocker; consider expert consult.
  6. Stable wide regular (likely VT) → amiodarone 150 mg IV over 10 min, repeatable; or procainamide or sotalol; expert consult.
  7. Polymorphic VT (Torsades) → magnesium sulfate 1–2 g IV; defibrillate if pulseless.

Post–Cardiac-Arrest Care (After ROSC)

  1. Optimize ventilation: Target SpO₂ 92–98%, EtCO₂ 35–45 mmHg. Avoid hyperventilation—reduces venous return and worsens cerebral perfusion.
  2. Hemodynamics: SBP > 90 / MAP > 65. Crystalloid bolus, then norepinephrine 0.1–0.5 mcg/kg/min titrated.
  3. 12-lead ECG: Identify STEMI → activate cath lab. PCI is appropriate even without obvious STEMI in many post-arrest patients (electrical-cause arrest with high suspicion for ACS).
  4. Targeted Temperature Management (TTM): 32–36 °C × 24 hours for comatose adults post-arrest with ROSC.
  5. Glycemic control: Treat hypoglycemia; avoid extreme hyperglycemia.
  6. Don't prognosticate early. Neurologic outcome cannot be reliably predicted in the first 72 hours.
Pearl. The lowest-yield drug in cardiac arrest is the one given without first verifying compression depth, ventilation rate, and rhythm. Drugs amplify good CPR; they do not replace it.

Case Study — Witnessed Collapse at the Gym

Dispatch

0915 hrs — Adult male, ~55 yrs, witnessed collapse in fitness center. Bystander CPR in progress. AED applied, "shock advised, shock delivered" per dispatcher relay. ETA 4 min.

Arrival

Patient supine on gym floor. Bystander performing compressions. AED pads in place. Patient: pulseless, apneic, cyanotic. AED rhythm: VF.

Initial ACLS Actions
  • Take over high-quality CPR, transition to your monitor/defibrillator.
  • Assign roles: leader, compressor (rotate q2 min), airway, IV/IO, recorder.
  • Continue CPR while pads switched. Capnography placed on BVM filter.

Team leader calls H's & T's: hypoxia (addressed via airway/O₂), hyper-/hypokalemia (no labs—history), hypothermia (no), tamponade (no JVD), tension PTX (BS equal), thrombosis (likely coronary—post-resus PCI plan), toxin (no overdose hx), acidosis (EtCO₂ trending up).

Cycle 4 — Rhythm Check

Sinus tachycardia at 110. Pulse check: palpable carotid. EtCO₂ jumped to 38 mmHg during last compressions—classic ROSC indicator.

Post-ROSC Plan
  • 12-lead ECG: anterior STEMI (V1–V4 ST elevation, reciprocal inferior depression).
  • SpO₂ 96% on FiO₂ 0.5—back off to maintain 92–98%.
  • BP 92/58 → norepinephrine infusion 0.1 mcg/kg/min titrated to MAP > 65.
  • Activate cath lab en route. Notify of prolonged downtime, post-arrest STEMI, hemodynamic support requirement.
  • Initiate TTM at receiving facility (32–36 °C × 24 hr) given comatose state.

Debrief Questions

  1. What was the EtCO₂ pattern that signaled ROSC, and why? (Sudden rise from ~18 to ~38 mmHg reflects restored pulmonary perfusion delivering CO₂ to alveoli.)
  2. Why amiodarone after the second shock and not the first? (Per algorithm: drugs follow the second unsuccessful shock, after epinephrine. The first shock + 2 min CPR may itself convert.)
  3. What single team-dynamics behavior most influenced this outcome? (Closed-loop communication on shock timing + uninterrupted compressions—compression fraction stayed > 80%.)
  4. When is the right time to discuss field termination of resuscitation? (After ALL reversible causes have been addressed and EtCO₂ remains < 10 mmHg after 20 minutes of high-quality ALS — and per local protocol.)
Section 5 · Summary & Key Takeaways

What Will Save Your Next Code

Five Anchors

  1. CPR is the medication. Drugs and shocks amplify good CPR; nothing replaces it.
  2. Capnography is your cockpit. < 10 = bad CPR or dead patient; rise to > 35 = ROSC; sudden drop = tube out.
  3. Algorithm fluency frees your brain. The mental space you save on memorized steps is what you spend hunting H's and T's.
  4. Closed-loop communication. Order → repeat-back → execute → confirm. No exceptions, even on small tasks.
  5. Post-arrest care is half the outcome. The patient survives the next 24 hours because of what you do in the first 60 minutes after ROSC.

Discussion Prompt — For Group

"What single role on a code do you feel least prepared to lead? Compressor, airway, drugs, or team leader? What would change that?"

Reflection Activity — Solo

List three H's or T's you've never actually treated in a code. For each, write the single field action you'd take if you suspected it tomorrow.

Closing. The codes that survive are not the dramatic ones. They are the ones run by a calm team that did the simple things—compressions, airway, electricity, drugs at the right minute—without drama, without ego, and without taking their hands off the patient. That is what ACLS is for.
